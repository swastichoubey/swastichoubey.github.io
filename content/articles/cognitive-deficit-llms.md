A couple months back, I had to write a one line follow-up email so I sat down to write it and was stumped. Instantly I opened Claude to take care of it but then thought it is just a single line, let me do it. I then took 3 minutes to write it after which I still pasted it in Claude to proof-read it. After I hit send, I was hit with an existential crisis because I used to be able to write a 1000 words easily, I had a fairly good score in TOEFL, and was very well versed in both emailing etiquette as well as the subject of the email so why the struggle to write a single line? This was the point where I realized that by offloading tasks to LLMs I was doing critical damage to myself because offloading isn't just convenient, it's substitutive and rather than extending your thinking, it replaces it.

During my undergrad, I was working on a project and I was stuck on a particular module. I spent hours understanding error messages, doing test runs and on Stack Overflow trying to debug the code till I managed to get it running. There is a certain thrill when you get a piece of code running after hours of work and in addition to that, I learned so much on Stack Overflow that I helped out 3 other people with their projects. A few years later, for my Advanced Problem Solving class, we were given a challenge question and told to submit it the next day. I went home, tried my hand at it but couldn't really figure it out so I left the house early in the morning, sat in a cafe across the campus and got to work with a pen and paper fleshing out what needs to be done, looking at algorithms and just trying again and again till I got all test cases to pass. Again, the thrill in that moment left me smiling a little too creepily for a public place.

Going back further, when I was in school I was a prolific reader which translated to being an enthusiastic writer. During exams, our essays would have a word limit of 1000 words and I would start struggling at the 750 words mark trying to wrap it up so I don't go over. This extended to both my language exams. I loved competing in extempores and debates because it involved researching topics and presenting your thoughts as well as defending them. Debates would involve deep research so you could handle questions from the interjectors and preparing for extempores was all about getting information quickly and structuring it so your argument is substantive. I ended up learning a lot from every competition I participated in.

The point of this rant was that a couple of years back when there was no AI, doing things took more time and a lot more effort but at the end of it you always came out having learned more accompanied by a sense of accomplishment. Cut to now, if I get an error while coding, copy and paste on Claude then copy the solution and paste it in the editor, rinse and repeat. Sending an email? Ask one of the LLMs to write one and paste it in gmail.

On deeper introspection I found that my skill atrophy extended to other avenues such as coding and research. So I went digging and I found that I wasn't the only one experiencing this. Here are some tweets and posts from people who came to a similar realization:

During my deep dive, I came across a study conducted by MIT, titled "Your Brain on ChatGPT". In this study they set up 3 groups and tasked them to write an essay. Group 1 had to use LLMs, Group 2 had to use a search engine and Group 3 had to do it independently. The researchers used Electroencephalography (EEG) to measure the brain signals of the participants and found the following:

- LLM users had significant difficulty recalling quotes from their essays with 83% being unable to do so and the numbers didn't improve much in follow-up sessions. On the other hand, nearly all Search Engine users and Brain only participants achieved near perfect quoting ability.
- The Brain only group was able to confidently claim ownership of their essays, the Search Engine group were a little less certain about ownership but stable around 70-90% of their essay but the LLM group had a very fragmented sense of ownership with some claiming 100%, some claiming partial credit (50-90%) while some outright refusing ownership. This suggests that the use of LLMs diminishes cognitive agency.
- With the help of EEG, they were able to determine that the Brain only group showed a much higher level of neural connectivity which correlated with stronger memory, greater semantic accuracy, and firmer ownership of written work. They demonstrated deeper learning outcomes and stronger identity with their output. The Search Engine group displayed moderate internalization, likely balancing effort with outcome. The LLM group, while benefiting from tool efficiency, showed weaker memory traces, reduced self-monitoring, and fragmented authorship.

It is a very thorough experiment with a well written report that I recommend you read, especially if you are well versed in neuroscience.

This study was further proof that over reliance on LLMs was damaging to our cognitive abilities. Done with my internet foray, I then took a look at the people around me. As a Product Manager, I am fortunate enough to interact with people on different teams including developers, creators, and executives. I observed them for 4 months during which our development team started using Cursor, encouraged by the executives in order to speed up development and our creative team was similarly encouraged to use image and video Generation Models. I was not happy with what I saw.

One of our senior developers was exceptional at coding and he was one of those people who always found a solution so I was sufficiently impressed with him. He was tasked with building a full scale application, completely independently within a very short duration and of course he took up the task. Adept at using Cursor, he started off really strong and everyone was quite impressed, including me, until I found that it was completely vibe coded. The reason it threw me off was because in my experience, vibe coding is great for frontend design work but not architectural knowledge. When pressure was being put on my team to deliver faster, I had to take a stand against it. The reason for the demand of speedy delivery was that they have access to the same tool that the senior developer has so they should deliver just as fast. I tried to argue that while the other application was great to look at, I have serious doubts about its robustness. My concerns were dismissed and the pressure on my team doubled so I put forth a condition to them: prove the robustness of the vibe coded application through a round of scale testing then I will capitulate to your requests. The scale testing failed at 15 and I almost pulled a Captain Holt in my feeling of vindication.

After this whole debacle I started working on a second project with another developer. Again, smart guy who knew how to get his job done. He was a lot older and much slower to adopt AI. He asked me to teach him how to use Claude Code and I complied. After 2 months he became completely reliant on the tool. If the limits were up, he was unable to proceed with his tasks.

My own experiences supplemented by my observations of other people using AI concerned me enough to do something about it. For the team, I took a few workshops to teach them how to vibe coded without resorting to copying and pasting. For myself, I picked up a pen and paper, literally. I had already decided to start writing articles about what I was learning or experimenting with. I tried to do it in Google Docs but the first few times, I would look something up, get a Gemini summary and copy and paste that so I started writing in my notebook instead and voilà, I easily churned out a 7 page article without resorting to any AI tool or doomscrolling while the AI tool worked. I am not demonizing AI, it is an incredible tool but it is meant to uplift and not just take on your daily tasks. Use it to understand concepts in a learning style suitable to you, let it help you with coding but understand the solution it proposes and implement it yourself. When I write an article on paper, I take photos of it and use OCR to convert it to text and then use AI to weed out any factual inaccuracies but make the edits myself so that I learn something too.

Let AI write your emails if you so wish but in the time you saved, do something enriching instead. You can catch up with news, read latest papers, pick up a book you have been meaning to read or explore more creative outlets.

My mom is a teacher and AI has had an adverse impact on children as well. The lethal combination of AI and the addictiveness of short form videos has made the attention span of children alarmingly short. AI is like a more advanced form of a calculator, I love Maths and had no issues solving integral equations but if there was a calculator in front of me during the exam, I would verify what 2+2 was. The problem is that AI is not only everywhere but also capable of a lot more tasks. While a calculator could only aid you, AI can just take over and complete the task on its own which is why these tools are almost addictive but like all addictions, this too is crippling.

Everyday more and more people are becoming aware of the damage that AI is doing to their cognitive skills and they are taking active measures to combat it. There are also just as many people falling to the charms of it and of course a third group who are indifferent to it. As I transitioned to AI Safety, I met so many people who are completely indifferent to AI, they just put their head down and do their work, using AI tools only in an experimental capacity or to offload menial tasks to it. A few years down the line there is going to be a divide between people who are completely dependent on AI and those that can hold their own in a conversation. I would very much like to be a part of the latter group.
