Xenobots are living, programmable organisms. That is a wild statement but it is true. These new creatures were designed on a supercomputer at the University of Vermont and then assembled and tested by biologists at Tufts University — appropriate given that xenobots are neither completely robotic nor a known animal species.

## What They Can Do

Apart from being genuinely strange, xenobots were designed to perform specific tasks: moving towards a target, or carrying medicine to a location inside a patient. Despite being less than a millimeter wide, these organisms can move, work together in groups, self-heal, and reproduce. That last one is the interesting part.

The reproduction mechanism differs from any other known biological reproduction. Xenobots use kinetic replication — they can create copies of themselves from any level of movement or action. This is not metaphor. The cells literally aggregate into new organisms through motion.

## The Obvious Follow-Up Question

Before any conspiracies arise: the researchers confirmed that these organisms were contained in the lab and, being biodegradable, can be easily deactivated. They are also regulated by ethics experts. The researchers were clearly aware that 'self-replicating organism' is a phrase that requires some immediate reassurance.

### Proposed applications

Collecting microplastics in the ocean, inspecting root systems of plants, and regenerative medicine. The common thread is tasks that require navigating spaces and scales that are difficult for conventional robotics — and where biodegradability is a feature rather than a limitation.

---

The thing that sticks with me is the design process: a supercomputer generating an anatomical blueprint, biologists assembling it from frog skin and heart muscle cells. The boundary between designed artifact and living organism is doing something unusual here.
